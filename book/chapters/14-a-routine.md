# Chapter 14: A Routine That Uses the Technology

*The published method is older than any of this software and does not need it. It isolates one skill at a time, tests against fixed standards, recombines the pieces into scenarios, and simulates distance by shrinking the target rather than the room. Inside that, a laser app earns its place in exactly two jobs.*

## Elements, standards, scenarios

Stoeger organises his drills into three kinds, and the names are his.<sup>[1]</sup>

**Elements** are isolation exercises. One thing at a time: trigger control, the draw, transitions, movement. The argument for them is practical. A thirty-round course contains grip, trigger, draw, reloads, transitions and movement all at once, and working all of it in every drill is inefficient. The structure came out of watching what helped the largest number of shooters in his classes.

**Standards** are known-distance, known-time drills, with times he supplies rather than times you set. His reason is that shooters worldwide use the same drills and times, so collectively they put the bar in a sensible place. One setup rule separates them from everything else: leave the targets where they are, because they are supposed to be standards. The clue is in the name.

**Scenarios** recombine the elements into the shapes a match actually produces, and here the instruction reverses. Do not replicate the diagram, replicate the *challenge*, in whatever space you have. You are not expected to shoot every scenario every day, only to rotate through them so everything gets worked at least a little each month.

One thing to get right, because the tidy version is wrong. There is no prescribed order. The parts are numbered and nowhere does he say to finish one layer before starting the next. What he does say is that if the Standards are giving you trouble, go back to the Elements, and that your emphasis should follow your goal: more Standards work to move up in classification, more Scenarios work to move better on field courses. Work on everything, and put special emphasis where you benefit most.

So it is three lenses rather than a staircase.

## Shrink the target, not the room

The mechanism that makes a home range possible is scaling, and the rule is arithmetic.

Multiply the target scale by the distance you want to simulate. Quarter-scale targets and a simulated ten yards means standing two and a half yards away, which is seven and a half feet. Almost nobody has ten yards of dry-fire space and virtually nobody has twenty-five, which is why every distance in his drills is prefixed with the word simulated.<sup>[2]</sup>

The caveat is the part people skip. Going too small is a real problem: once you are close enough to a tiny target it becomes hard to pull your eye back to the front sight, so stay about five feet from the target regardless of scale. That is a floor on the whole technique, and it is about your eye rather than about the target.

Note what the rule is for. It is printed targets at a measured distance. Scaling a silhouette drawn on a screen is a different calculation, and several products in this report draw scaled competition silhouettes without publishing the geometry behind them, so the distance a screen claims to simulate is worth treating as a label rather than a measurement.

## Sessions

Fifteen to thirty minutes, about five minutes per drill, done daily, and five days a week is common.<sup>[3]</sup> Most people manage somewhere between five and fifteen minutes, and carrying a small number of goals into a session beats carrying a long list.<sup>[4]</sup>

That is the whole of it. Short and done beats long and planned.

On pushing hard enough, there is one sentence that applies directly to anyone using a scoring app: "If you are 'perfect,' you aren't pushing and thus aren't advancing your skill level."<sup>[3]</sup> It is the same principle as a par time you should sometimes miss, and it applies to any product that shows you a number.

## When to bring the timer in, and when to take it away

This is a sequence worth following exactly, and it bears on every app in this report.

For somebody brand new, no timer. Learn the motions, work out the draw and the reload, fix the big problems, and start working with the timer once you are smooth and consistent. And if you decide to change a technique, take the timer away again, focus on the change, look at video of yourself, and only then bring the time pressure back.<sup>[5]</sup>

Read that as a rule about measurement in general rather than about clocks. Every product in this report is a measuring instrument, and there are stages at which a measurement interferes with learning the thing being measured.

## Where the technology fits

What the apps genuinely add to this method is two things, and chapters 9 and 10 established both. A recorded hit position, where the method otherwise asks you to judge your own sight picture honestly and count an unsure call as a miss. And a measured time, where the method's own timer only beeps.

Where to set the dot aside follows from chapter 1. Stoeger's objection is conditional on training sight focus, and he permits the laser as a supplement. Cheng rates it specifically for trigger control.<sup>[6, 4]</sup> So use the dot for the drills where an objective hit helps, and put it away for the ones where you are training what your eye does.

The method was complete before any of this existed, which is the argument for treating the technology as an instrument inside it rather than a replacement for it.

## Key takeaways

- Three lenses, not a staircase: elements, standards, scenarios.
- Scale the target rather than the room, and stay about five feet back.
- Fifteen to thirty minutes, daily, with a few goals at a time.
- If you are perfect, you are not pushing hard enough.
- Timers come in after the basics and out when you change technique.
- The apps add a recorded hit and a measured time, and nothing beyond that.
