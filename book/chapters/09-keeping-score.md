# Chapter 9: Keeping Score

*Every product here gives you a hit position and a time. Beyond that the numbers diverge, and two products' scores for the same string of shots are not the same number. Score yourself against yourself, and establish the one rule that matters by firing at it.*

## What a score is made of

Three ingredients, in every product in this report.

**Where the hit landed**, as a position on a target face. **Which zone or ring that position falls in**, as a number. And **when it happened**, which chapter 10 is about.

LaserHIT publishes the most detailed account of its own scoring of any vendor here, and the shape is representative: a score per hit, a total, and a time to go with it.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Hit factor, and the one vendor who writes it down

Competition shooters measure by hit factor, which is points divided by time. It is the number that makes a fast sloppy string comparable with a slow precise one.

LaserHIT states the arithmetic plainly: hit factor is total score divided by total time.<sup>[1]</sup> iShooter and PlinkPal both name hit factor as a feature without setting out the arithmetic.<sup>[2, 3]</sup>

No vendor names a rulebook edition. Where a discipline appears, it appears as a style rather than a standard: ISSF is referenced as a style or an inspiration, and the practical-shooting abbreviations turn up as mode names rather than as compliance claims.<sup>[1, 2, 3]</sup>

Treat that as the useful information it is. A product advertising hit factor is offering you the competition shooter's arithmetic. It is not offering you a scored stage that would stand up at a match, and it does not claim to be.

## The boundary rule, and how to find it yourself

Here is the one thing worth testing before you trust a score.

A shot that lands on a scoring line has to go one way or the other, and the product decides which. That decision is not in the documentation of any product in this report, and it changes a close string's total.

You can settle it in two minutes. Fire deliberately at a ring line, several times, and watch what the app awards. Do it at the boundary that matters to you, which on most targets is the one between the two highest rings. Whatever it gives you is the rule, and it is now a known quantity for your setup.

That is the general move for everything in this chapter. The numbers these products produce are measurements of something: the question is always of what, and a few deliberate shots will tell you more than any specification.

## Where the score lives, and what it costs

The thing a scoring product is for can be the thing you have to buy.

Splits and hit scores sit behind the paid tier in two of the products here, and chapter 11 sets out the shapes those tiers take. Check which one carries the number you actually want before you judge a product by its free version.

## What the training method asks of a score

The published method sets a stricter standard than any app, and it is worth holding alongside the numbers.

Stoeger's rule is that you judge the shot by the sight picture you actually saw, and that an unsure call counts as a miss.<sup>[4]</sup> A camera cannot do that. It reports where the dot landed, which is a different question from whether you saw what you needed to see.

Hold both, because they fail in opposite directions. Your own judgement is generous to you and the camera is indifferent to you. The pair of them together is better information than either alone.

## Using the numbers

Three habits make the scores useful rather than decorative.

**Compare yourself against yourself.** Within one product, on one setup, over weeks, the trend is real. Across two products it is not a comparison at all.

**Separate the score from the time.** A total that improves because you slowed down is not an improvement, which is exactly what hit factor exists to expose.

**Keep the hit positions, not just the totals.** If you want group measures such as extreme spread or mean radius, you will be working them out from the positions yourself, and the positions are the part worth exporting.

## Key takeaways

- Every product gives a hit position, a ring value and a time.
- Two products' scores for the same string are not the same number.
- Hit factor is points divided by time, and one vendor publishes that.
- Fire at a ring line to learn which way yours rounds.
- Splits and hit scores can sit behind a paid tier.
- Keep the hit positions rather than only the totals, because the positions are the part worth exporting.
