# Chapter 1: Dry Fire Works, and the Dot Is Controversial

*Dry fire, practice with an unloaded gun and no ammunition, is the most efficient way to build gun handling and trigger control. The laser is a later addition, and the two instructors this report draws on reach opposite verdicts on the same laser device. That disagreement is the frame you need to read a product page without being sold to.*

## Nobody argues about the empty gun

Start with what is settled, because it is short.

Stoeger's case for dry fire is an efficiency argument. He trained shooters on live fire only, dry fire only, and a combination, and the combination won every time.<sup>[1]</sup> He gives the arrangement a name, the live-fire/dry-fire loop.<sup>[2]</sup> You decide what you want to do, you build it in thousands of repetitions without ammunition, you go shoot live rounds to find out whether it worked, and you change the dry work to match what the live rounds told you.

Cheng arrives in the same place from a different direction. Dry fire is essential and it is cheap, because it costs no ammunition and needs no special equipment.<sup>[3]</sup> He puts it to work on the fundamentals, trigger control above all, and he is clear that the accessories are optional. Snap caps and laser trainers are not required to dry fire.

Two instructors, two different audiences, same answer. Practice with the empty gun.

Then somebody adds a laser, and the agreement stops.

## The Dot Problem

Name it now, because it runs through every chapter after this one.

**The Dot Problem** is that a laser paints a bright mark on the target, and the mark is not the thing you are supposed to be looking at. A pistol shooter training **sight focus** holds optical focus on the front sight, with the target deliberately soft. A bright dot out there competes for that focus, and it wins, because it is the brightest thing in the room.

Stoeger raises exactly that objection. He names inserted laser cartridges and the SIRT training pistol, calls them potentially problematic for practical shooters, and ties the objection to one condition: the distraction applies if you are training yourself to have a sight focus.<sup>[4]</sup> His own sentence is the one most often quoted: "I don't know anyone that puts up with the distraction of the laser over the long term."<sup>[4]</sup>

Two things usually get lost when that line travels on its own. He permits the device, writing that you can certainly use the laser to supplement regular dry fire. And he sets an end state rather than a ban: eventually you have to learn to read the sights yourself. The laser is scaffolding, and scaffolding comes down.

So he is not hostile to the technology. *He is conditionally skeptical about it, in one paragraph, about a device he says you may use.*

One terminology trap, because it will bite you elsewhere in his book. Stoeger writes about a "dot" constantly, and most of the time he means a fiber-optic front sight or a red-dot optic on the gun. Different dot, different place, and it is the one he wants you looking at.

## The same pistol, the opposite verdict

Cheng likes the laser. He practises fundamentals at home with laser systems and is specific about which fundamental benefits: "Focusing on trigger control is a great use of lasers, as the laser will jump if you have any erratic movements."<sup>[5]</sup> He names the SIRT pistol, calls it an excellent dry-fire option, and says he uses it all the time at home.

That is the same device Stoeger names. One instructor has never met anyone who tolerated it, and the other uses it continuously. Both are describing their own practice, so the disagreement is real and there is no test that settles it.

What makes it interesting rather than merely contradictory is where Cheng stands on focus. He teaches front-sight focus firmly, with the target and the rear sight blurry. He holds the position Stoeger says the dot interferes with, and uses the dot anyway.

The useful conclusion is that whether a laser helps depends on what you are training in that session. For trigger control, where the dot's jump is the feedback, it helps. For sight focus, where the dot pulls your eye off the front sight, it works against you.

## The gap the laser is aimed at

Stoeger describes a real weakness in dry fire. In his method you hold yourself accountable for the sight picture you saw, and if you are not sure you had it, you call it a miss. He also notes that many shooters are not honest with themselves about those sight pictures, which produces fast dry-fire times and inconsistent results with live ammunition.<sup>[4]</sup>

That is a feedback problem, named by the skeptic, and it is precisely the hole laser hit-detection claims to fill. Neither book discusses cameras, screens or scoring software, so the connection is this report's to make and the chapters that follow are where it gets tested.

What the laser adds is a candidate answer to a problem the training literature already identified. What it risks is the thing that literature already warned about. Hold both.

The dot is a tool for finding out whether you can do the thing without it.

## Key takeaways

- Dry fire is efficient, cheap and needs no equipment, and that part is settled.
- The laser helps trigger control, because an erratic press makes the dot jump.
- It fights sight focus, because the dot is brighter than your front sight.
- Match the tool to the session rather than adopting or rejecting it wholesale.
- Dry fire's weakness is feedback: you are the judge of your own sight picture.
