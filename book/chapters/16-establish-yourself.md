# Chapter 16: What You Will Have to Establish Yourself

*Some of what decides whether a setup suits you is not in anyone's documentation, and you can settle every item on this list in an evening with the gun you already own. Each one is a measurement rather than a question, and each takes minutes.*

## How your product treats a line

Chapter 9 explains why this one matters. Fire deliberately at a ring boundary a dozen times and write down what the app awards, because that rule is the single largest unknown in any score you will compare over time.

## What your room does to the detector

Two measurements, and neither takes more than a minute of your time.

**With nothing firing**, let the app watch your target for a minute and see whether it reports anything at all. Detections with no trigger pull are the room, not the gun, and the usual culprits are a window, a lamp, a television standby light and anything reflective in the camera's view. Chapter 8 is the order to work through.

**With the light as it will be**, fire ten shots and count how many register. Do it in the light you actually practise in, not with the curtains open for the test. The number you get is the only detection rate that matters to you, and no specification anywhere will give it to you.

Repeat the second measurement at the far corners of your target or screen, because the edges are where a setup fails first.

## How bright your cartridge is

The products that work with any visible laser will work better with a brighter one, and brightness varies more between cartridges than any product page suggests.

Fire the same ten shots with each cartridge you own and compare the counts. If one cartridge registers consistently worse, that is your answer, and it is cheaper to replace a cartridge than to abandon an app. Keep infrared out of it entirely: two of the products in this report rule it out explicitly, and a detector built for visible red cannot see it.

## Whether your screen surface works

For the screen and projector group, the surface is equipment and the vendors do not agree about it, so this one has to be settled in your own living room.

Try the product on the surface you have before you buy film for it. Then try it with the film your vendor specifies. The difference will be obvious in the first ten shots, and the right answer is whichever one detects your shots, not whichever one the category recommends.

## How long your setup actually takes

Time it once, honestly, from walking into the room to the first scored shot. Include the tripod, the calibration and the target.

That number is the one chapter 12 says decides whether you keep practising, and it is the number most worth improving. Anything you can leave assembled, leave assembled. Anything you have to rebuild each evening is a candidate for replacement by something simpler.

## Whether the thing is still being maintained

Look at the app listing's version history rather than at the marketing. A listing that says new and improved while its last update is years old has told you something worth knowing, and it takes about thirty seconds to check.

## What your own group looks like

This is the last item and the most useful one over time.

Keep the hit positions rather than the totals, for the reasons chapter 9 gives, and compare yourself against yourself inside one product. Then keep the clock and the score apart, so that a total which improved because you slowed down can never read back to you as progress.

## Key takeaways

- Fire at a ring line and watch which way your product rounds it.
- Measure your room twice, once silent and once with ten shots.
- Compare your cartridges against each other, and leave infrared out.
- Settle the screen surface in your own room before buying film.
- Time your setup from the door to the first scored shot.
- Read an app's version history rather than its marketing copy.
- Keep the hit positions, and compare yourself against yourself rather than against somebody else's product.
