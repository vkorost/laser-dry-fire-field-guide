# Chapter 6: Webcams, Projectors and Screens

*Putting the target on a screen buys you targets that move, drills that score themselves and a timer that starts itself. It costs you a target that emits its own light into the camera, and it makes the surface of your screen part of the equipment. Three vendors give three different instructions about that surface, and they cannot all be right for your room.*

## Four arrangements

**Dry Fire Online** draws targets in a web browser and watches the screen with a phone or tablet running its camera app. It does not support USB webcams and it does not support infrared lasers. The camera may sit left or right, above or below the screen, provided all four corners of the display stay in view.<sup>[1]</sup>

**Interactive Gun Range** is a free-to-play game on Steam, released on 29 July 2026, which uses a webcam to watch the display directly. On a phone or tablet the device's own camera does the watching instead. It tells you not to buy an infrared laser.<sup>[2]</sup>

**Laser Ammo's Smokeless Range** ships its own USB tracking camera with the software, and you supply the projector and a Windows computer.<sup>[3]</sup>

**iMarksman** is built around a projector, with a calibration sensor camera, an infrared filter and an infrared training handgun, and you provide the projector and screen.<sup>[4]</sup>

Those are four different answers to one question, which is how to get a camera to see a dot on a surface that is already glowing.

## The surface is equipment

This is the part people discover after buying. Take it first.

A projector screen reflects the dot back at the camera. A television or monitor emits its own light and reflects very little, which is why every screen-based product here has something to say about the surface, and why what they say differs.

**Interactive Gun Range** is the one product here that puts targets on an ordinary computer monitor and says so. Asked whether a projector is needed, its FAQ answers: no, although a projector gives the best experience, modern computer monitors usually have matte displays and will work, albeit with small targets; a television works if its surface is matte, or if it is glossy and an anti-glare film has been applied.<sup>[2]</sup> Note the condition doing the work in that sentence -- *matte*. Its own Steam listing is narrower and mentions only a television with a matte screen protector or a projector, so a buyer reading the store page alone would not learn that a monitor is an option at all.<sup>[2]</sup>

**Dry Fire Online** says the opposite about anti-glare: anti-glare and polarizing filters on flat screens reduce the visibility of the laser dot, and it recommends a thin frosted film instead.<sup>[1]</sup>

**Laser Ammo** recommends a white projection screen, and for a flat-screen television its setup guide requires a filter material taped to the screen in order to track the laser; the material it suggests is a vinyl tablecloth. The guide also explains why, in a sentence that is the clearest statement of the optics anywhere in this category: some televisions will only reflect the laser at an angle matching the angle at which the laser entered the screen, so the camera cannot see it. And it concedes a case it cannot solve -- a television whose protective layer sits above the screen surface blurs the image under the filter, and there is no way to fix it.<sup>[3]</sup>

Three vendors, three surfaces: anti-glare film, frosted film, vinyl sheet. **Follow the documentation of the product you own.** But the disagreement is not as arbitrary as it looks, and the variable that resolves it is not the surface at all.

## Where the camera stands decides which surface works

Put the camera **square on to the screen**, and a glossy panel is a help rather than a hindrance: the mirror reflection of the dot comes straight back along the line it arrived on, which is roughly where the camera is. A matte coating spreads that same light over a wide angle, so less of it returns to any one place. This is the world Dry Fire Online describes when it blames anti-glare coatings for a dot the camera cannot see, and it recommends the camera be placed directly in front of the screen.<sup>[1]</sup>

Put the camera **off to one side**, and it inverts. The mirror reflection now leaves on the far side of the screen and never reaches the lens, so a glossy panel goes dark to the camera while a matte one keeps scattering light in every direction, including towards it. Laser Ammo's sentence above describes exactly this failure, and the film they sell is a way of forcing a glossy television to behave like a matte one.

So the two pieces of advice that look contradictory are each correct for a different camera position, and neither vendor says so, because each is describing the only arrangement it supports. For a buyer the rule is short: **the further off-axis your camera sits, the more the surface has to be matte.**

## Why a glowing target is the hard case

What the camera has to do is pick out a small red mark on a surface that is already producing light of its own. A screen makes that harder in two ways at once.

The screen is lit, so the dot has less contrast to work with than it would on paper. And whatever the software draws is in the camera's view, so a reddish graphic is a candidate hit. That is why screen-based products are fussy about room light and about what colours they put on screen, and chapter 8 is the account of what it costs.

The practical consequence for a buyer is that a screen setup has more ways to go wrong and more adjustment available when it does. A phone on paper either sees the target or does not. A screen setup can see the target, see the dot, and still score a reflection off the bezel.

## Nobody tells you where to put the camera

For a category whose whole function is a camera looking at a surface, the documentation on where that camera goes is remarkably thin.

Only **Dry Fire Online** states a rule: anywhere, provided all four corners of the display stay in view, with extreme angles costing precision at the corners.<sup>[1]</sup> **Laser Ammo** gives practical advice rather than a limit, suggesting the camera sit in front of the television at least level with its bottom edge, and offering a tripod to one side looking down as a remedy for ceiling-light reflections.<sup>[3]</sup> **Interactive Gun Range** says nothing at all: its FAQ, its setup page, its download page and its store listing contain no guidance on camera position, angle or distance.<sup>[2]</sup>

That silence is worth noticing, because it is the one variable the previous section shows to be decisive. A product that does not tell you where to stand the camera is not necessarily indifferent to it.

## What the screen buys you

Everything above is a cost, so be clear about the return.

Targets that move, which paper cannot do at all. Drills that reset themselves between strings. A clock that starts when the target appears rather than when you press something. Scenarios rather than a single target face. And on the projector systems, a target the size of a wall, which is the only arrangement in this report that gets close to the apparent size of a real range.

That is a genuine difference in what you can practise, not a cosmetic one. Chapter 14 is the method it has to fit inside.

## Key takeaways

- Four arrangements, all of them watching a surface that is already lit.
- The screen surface is equipment, not a setting.
- Three vendors ask for three different films, so follow the one that came with yours.
- The further off-axis the camera sits, the more the surface has to be matte.
- Only one of these products documents where the camera may be placed.
- Dry Fire Online rules out USB webcams and infrared lasers.
- A lit screen is the hardest detection case in this report.
- What you get back is movement, self-scoring drills and a self-starting clock.
