# Chapter 12: What Keeps People Practicing

*Shooters abandon these products when getting started takes longer than the session they had in mind, rather than because the drills run out. Everything else that helps is secondary to that, and the two things most worth buying are a trigger that resets itself and a target that scores itself.*

## The setup tax

People stop when tripod placement, calibration, lighting changes or target loading take longer than the practice they were planning.

The clearest sign of it is what they do next. Shooters kept the laser cartridge and stopped using the camera app, shooting instead at household objects or simple electronic targets.<sup>[1]</sup> They did not give up dry fire. They gave up the part that had to be assembled.

That is **the setup tax**, and it is paid every single time, against a session that chapter 14 puts at fifteen to thirty minutes. One owner put it exactly: by the time the setup was done, there was little interest left in using the app.<sup>[2]</sup>

Whatever else these products compete on, what decides whether anybody keeps using them is how long it takes to start.

## Immediate feedback is what makes repetition bearable

The strongest thing on the other side of the ledger is also the simplest.

Shooters credit visible hit confirmation and audible feedback, a gunshot or a steel sound, with making repetitive practice interesting enough to continue. One reported that reactive targets kept interest high and led to very few missed sessions compared with unassisted dry fire.<sup>[1]</sup>

This is worth more than it looks, because it is the one thing dry fire without equipment cannot give you at all. In the unassisted version you press the trigger and nothing happens, which is exactly the problem chapter 1 describes: you are the judge of your own sight picture, and you are not a reliable one.

## Visible progress, structure and variety

Three further supports, in the order the evidence puts them.

**A visible record.** Shooters who can see score history and grouping over time credit it with keeping them going. The improvement reports are their own, rather than measured results, but the mechanism is familiar from every other kind of training log.<sup>[1, 2]</sup>

**Structured courses.** A sequence with a test at the end and something tangible for passing it gets credited by reviewers, mostly on the vendors' own curricula.<sup>[2]</sup>

**Variety of drills.** Mentioned, and mixed. Nobody stops because they ran out of drills, which is the quiet implication of the setup tax sitting at the top of the list.

Streaks and leaderboards are the features vendors talk about most and shooters mention least, so the first three are where the money belongs.

## The trigger that resets itself

This is the one mechanical detail worth acting on, because it is the top-ranked item you can actually buy.

A striker-fired pistol has to be racked after every trigger press. In a dry-fire session that is a second or two of handling between every repetition, and shooters credit devices that reset the trigger with keeping them practising. One vendor's own manual calls the need to rack the slide after every shot a big problem for laser training.<sup>[2]</sup>

The training literature got there first and did not solve it. Stoeger describes a rubber band that holds the gun out of battery so the trigger can be pressed repeatedly, notes that the trigger then feels squishy, and says plainly that there is no good solution.<sup>[3]</sup>

Note which friction that is. It belongs to the gun rather than to the software, and it is there whether or not a camera is watching.

## What this means if you are choosing

The two highest-ranked things you can buy are a device that resets the trigger and a target that scores itself, and chapter 7 covers the second.

Both work the same way: they remove a step between you and the next repetition. That is the entire mechanism behind every finding in this chapter.

A setup that stays assembled beats a better app you have to rebuild each evening.

## Key takeaways

- Shooters abandon the setup rather than the practice.
- Immediate feedback, visible and audible, is what makes repetition bearable.
- Then a visible record, then structure, then variety.
- Streaks and leaderboards get the marketing and not the mentions.
- Buy a deleted step: a trigger that resets, a target that scores.
