# Chapter 8: Why Your Trainer Misses

*Three complaints recur across this category, and they are the same three whichever product you buy. The app records hits that never happened, it misses hits that did, and it puts real hits in the wrong place. Each has a short list of usual causes, and the sensitivity slider is the last of them rather than the first.*

## Hits that never happened

Owners of Mantis, Strikeman, G-Sight and iTarget all report false hits from reflections, ceiling lights, shadows and red indicator lights.<sup>[1]</sup> iTarget owners mention ceiling lights, reddish light and shadows by name, along with having to retune sensitivity when the light changes. Strikeman owners report several false hits a second under some indoor lighting and phantom bursts under certain compact fluorescent bulbs.<sup>[1]</sup>

Four products, four companies, one complaint. That consistency is the useful part, because it means the cause is the arrangement rather than any one firm's software.

**Reflections are not hits**, and the reason this keeps happening is that nothing in the camera's view announces itself as a laser. The app is looking for a small bright red mark, and a ceiling light reflected in a glossy surface, a red charging indicator, or a reddish patch of evening sun gives it exactly that.

There is one free diagnostic, and it settles the question before you change any setting. Let the app watch your target while nobody is shooting. Anything it records in that minute came from the room, and the fix is in the room rather than in the app. Chapter 5 carries the vendor instruction that covers most of what is usually there.

On a screen setup there is one more light source in the frame, and it is the display itself. A camera cannot tell red light from your laser apart from red light the software put on screen, which is why the screen-based products are specific about room light and about what they draw.

The vendors treat all of this as yours to tune, and chapter 3 lists the controls they hand you. Those controls are useful once the room is sorted out and misleading before it, because a threshold chosen against a reflection will be the wrong threshold for a laser.

## Hits that did not register

Here the usual cause is the cheapest component in the whole arrangement.

**The cartridge.** Interactive Gun Range's own guidance is to buy the brightest system available, preferably one that resets itself, and its developer reports that the inexpensive cartridges tried were too dim to register at all.<sup>[2]</sup> Owners across the category describe loose or unevenly seated cartridges, laser and sight offset, emitters that dim over time and cartridges that fail outright.<sup>[1]</sup>

Two owner reports put a number on what a dim emitter costs. One Dry Fire Online user reported a particular cartridge being detected about 15 per cent of the time, and one G-Sight user reported about 20 per cent.<sup>[1]</sup> Those are single accounts rather than a measured rate for either product, and they are the right order of magnitude to take seriously, because this failure arrives looking exactly like broken software.

**The light in the room.** Strikeman owners report glitches in poor light and needing plenty of it.<sup>[1]</sup> That pulls against the instinct to darken the room, and the two are reconciled by what each product is watching. A camera reading a printed target needs enough light to see the paper, while a camera watching a lit display is already receiving plenty.

**Exposure.** Several of these products have an exposure or sensitivity step of their own, listed in chapter 3. Run it in the light you actually practise in rather than the light you happened to set up in, and run it again when the light changes.

**The surface.** For anything watching a screen, the film or sheet the vendor specifies is part of the detection chain rather than an accessory, and chapter 6 sets out why three vendors ask for three different ones.

## Hits in the wrong place

This is the failure that reads most like a broken detector and usually is not one.

Dry Fire Online's manual states it plainly: move the camera and you have to calibrate again.<sup>[3]</sup> Owners supply the rest of the picture. iTarget users report automatic and manual target setup failing, with hits delayed or misplaced. One Dry Fire Online user reported hits registering well off target on a television. Smokeless Range owners report hours spent calibrating, and that a television worked less well than a projector.<sup>[1]</sup>

There is a reading rule that separates the two cases, and it costs you one string of deliberate shots.

If the holes are displaced the same way every time, further left or consistently low, the geometry is wrong. The camera has moved, or the calibration is stale, and no amount of sensitivity will correct it. If the holes are scattered at random around where you aimed, the problem is at the other end, in the dot and how brightly it is arriving.

Which of the four answers in chapter 3 your product uses decides how much work that costs you. A marker-based setup re-aligns itself. A setup you align by hand needs aligning again.

## A diagnostic order

Work down this list rather than starting at the slider.

- **Is it recording hits with nobody shooting?** If so the cause is in the room, so deal with the lights, the mirrors and the glossy surfaces first.
- **Is the surface what your vendor asked for?** Every screen-based product in chapter 6 specifies one, and they differ.
- **Is the cartridge bright, visible rather than infrared, and properly seated?** This is the commonest cause of a trigger pull that leaves nothing behind.
- **Has the product's own exposure or sensitivity step been run in tonight's light?** Several offer one, and it is meant to be re-run.
- **Have you moved the camera since you calibrated?** Consistently displaced holes point here, and randomly scattered ones point at the cartridge.

## Key takeaways

- Three complaints recur: invented hits, missed hits, hits in the wrong place.
- The same complaint across four companies means the arrangement rather than the software.
- Owners report ceiling lights, reflections, shadows and red indicator lights as hits.
- A dim or badly seated cartridge is the commonest cause of a vanished shot.
- Displaced holes point at geometry, while scattered holes point at the dot.
- Work down the list in order, because the sensitivity slider is the last thing worth touching rather than the first.
